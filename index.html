The 10 kg Project

Evidence-based recomposition: 4 core lifting days, an optional specialization day, and everything needed to lose ~10 kg of fat while getting visibly more muscular.

Training

Tap a day, log every set. Values autosave to this browser. All exercises below are already shoulder- and elbow-adjusted — see the Shoulder & Elbows tab for the reasoning and the load-management rules.

Scheduling: Saturday and Sunday are always rest. In a 4-day week: Mon Upper A · Tue Lower A · Thu Upper B · Fri Lower B (Wednesday off). In a 5-day week, Wednesday becomes the Delt & Arm Day. Missed a session? Slide it into any free weekday — the pattern matters, not the weekday. Switch the week type on the Today tab or in Data.

Progression rules (double progression)

  1. Own the top of the rep range at the target RIR on every set → next session add 2.5 kg (upper) or 5 kg (lower) and reset to the bottom of the range.
  2. Added load but missed the bottom of the range → stay there until you own it.
  3. Same load/reps for 3 sessions → add a set, tweak technique, or swap to the substitute for 4 weeks.
  4. Shoulder or elbow talking to you? Train at RIR 3–4 that week, cut the sets that provoke it in half, keep every rep pain-free (≤3/10 and settling within 24 h). Pain is a load-management signal, not a character test.

Seven rules that do 90% of the work

  1. Protein at every meal, 40–50 g — even distribution beats one huge evening meal.
  2. Pre-log, don't post-log. Decide tomorrow's food tonight.
  3. Weigh food for two weeks, then eyeball forever.
  4. Same breakfast, same lunch most days. Boring is under-rated.
  5. Liquid calories are the enemy — juice, sweetened coffee, arak nights. One night out can erase three days of deficit.
  6. Restaurant strategy: grilled meat + salad + rice; skip fried food, toum and the bread basket.
  7. One planned flexible meal per week — portioned, enjoyed, then straight back on.

Weeks 1–2 are the most important weeks in the plan. Whey protein — mechanism first, then the verdict

How it actually works

  1. Fast delivery. Whey empties the stomach in ~20–30 min, producing the sharpest rise in plasma amino acids of any common protein, peaking ~60–90 min.
  2. The leucine trigger. A 20–40 g dose delivers ~2.5–3 g of leucine — the threshold for maximal muscle protein synthesis stimulation in young adults. Leucine is sensed by the protein Sestrin2, which releases its inhibition of GATOR2, allowing mTORC1 to switch on translation initiation in muscle.
  3. Substrate + insulin. Whey supplies the complete set of essential amino acids needed to build myofibrillar protein, and its insulinogenic amino acids give a modest insulin rise that supports amino acid uptake.
  4. The result: MPS rises 2–3× above fasting for ~2–3 h, then falls back — the "muscle-full" phenomenon. That's precisely why four feedings beat one giant evening meal: each feeding only buys a few hours of signal.
  5. Where it fails: if total daily protein is already adequate, extra whey adds almost nothing. It closes a gap; it doesn't open a bonus.

The evidence, graded

  • Strong Protein supplementation + resistance training increases lean mass (~+0.6 kg pooled) and modestly increases strength; the effect is much larger when baseline intake is under ~1.6 g/kg/day.
  • Strong Hypertrophy plateaus near 1.6 g/kg/day in energy balance.
  • Strong In a deficit, 2.2–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass preserves lean tissue far better than low protein — one trial had lifters at 2.4 g/kg gaining lean mass while losing fat.
  • Moderate Protein timing barely matters alone; total intake and distribution across 3–5 feedings dominate. The "anabolic window" is hours-to-a-day wide, not 30 minutes.
  • Strong Higher-protein diets are safe for kidneys in healthy people.
  • Not supported Isolate vs concentrate vs hydrolysate differences are trivial at matched leucine. BCAAs, mass gainers and collagen are not substitutes.
Verdict: yes, as a tool. 190 g of protein inside 2,200 kcal is genuinely hard with Lebanese food alone when a hospital day swallows your lunch. 1–2 scoops (25–40 g each, ~110–160 kcal) is the cheapest way to protect the protein number. Concentrate is fine unless lactose-sensitive; choose third-party tested (Informed Sport / NSF). It's a convenience product, not a requirement — 250 g of 0% labneh ≈ 24 g protein, a can of tuna ≈ 25–30 g. Don't buy BCAAs.

Creatine — and the creatinine trap

Language matters here, and you'll use it with patients: the supplement is creatine; creatinine is its metabolic waste product that we measure in serum to estimate GFR. Confusing the two generates half of the kidney myths in this category.

How it actually works

  1. The phosphagen system. Your ATP pool holds ~2–3 seconds of maximal work. Creatine kinase transfers a phosphate from phosphocreatine to ADP: PCr + ADP → Cr + ATP — essentially instantaneous, and what powers the first 5–10 seconds of every heavy set.
  2. More PCr = more work. Supplementing raises muscle PCr stores ~20%. Faster ATP regeneration between reps and between sets means more reps per set, faster inter-set recovery, and more high-quality volume per session.
  3. Buffering and shuttling. PCr buffers intramuscular H⁺ (creatine users often report fewer cramps — the cramp myth is backwards), and mitochondrial creatine kinase shuttles phosphates between mitochondria and myofibrils.
  4. Hypertrophy is indirect but real. Creatine doesn't directly build muscle: it raises training performance → more mechanical tension and volume → more growth. Secondary signals include intracellular cell swelling and possible effects on satellite cells and protein breakdown. Headline trials: ~+1–2 kg lean mass and 5–10% more strength versus placebo + training over 4–12 weeks.

The evidence, graded

  • Very strong The most effective legal ergogenic supplement for high-intensity capacity, strength, power and lean mass alongside resistance training (ISSN position stand, 2017 — not overturned).
  • Strong 3 g/day is enough; loading only saturates faster (5 days vs ~28), it doesn't raise the ceiling.
  • Strong (null) Time of day, with/without food, training vs rest days — none of it matters. Saturation is the point.
  • Strong Safe for kidneys, liver and muscle in healthy people, without dehydration or cramping; studied up to 5 years. A 2025 meta-analysis (21 studies): modest rise in serum creatinine, no change in measured or estimated GFR.
  • Insufficient data Pre-existing kidney disease or reduced eGFR, pregnancy, adolescents: caution, not alarm.
The creatinine trap. Creatine degrades to creatinine at ~1.6–2% of the pool per day. Add 5 g/day and you increase the pool, so serum creatinine rises ~0.05–0.2 mg/dL — enough to push a normal 1.0 toward 1.2 and "create" stage 2–3 CKD out of an eGFR equation. Direct GFR, cystatin C, proteinuria and albuminuria all stay normal. Tell any clinician taking bloods, or ask for cystatin C. And as a future doctor: never diagnose renal impairment on serum creatinine alone in a muscular young man who lifts or takes creatine. Same trap applies to post-leg-day CK, AST and ALT — draw labs 48–72 h after your last session.
Verdict: take it. Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, every day including rest days, timed however you'll remember. Don't bother loading. Skip HCl, buffered and nitrate forms — no demonstrated superiority, 3–10× the price. ~$5–10/month, the best evidence-to-price ratio in this whole document. Expect +1–2 kg of intracellular water in weeks 1–2: the muscle looks fuller because water moved into the cell.
Also worth your money: caffeine 3–6 mg/kg (~250–400 mg) 30–60 min pre-training — excellent evidence, no meaningful downside in a healthy 23-year-old, just not after 14:00, because sleep is your most anabolic supplement. Everything else (fat burners, test boosters, BCAAs, glutamine, SARMs) ranges from useless to dangerous.

The honest caveat

No trial has ever compared this exact program to StrongLifts in a 23-year-old Lebanese medical student. What I've done is more defensible: taken the variables with meta-analytic support — weekly volume, frequency, proximity to failure, range of motion, protein dose, rate of weight loss, supplement efficacy, concurrent-training sequencing — and assembled them inside your real constraints. Your logbook is the experiment; the primary outcomes are strength on four lifts, waist circumference, and body weight over 20 weeks.

Shoulder & elbows

Your history — right shoulder discomfort plus soreness in both elbows after heavy sessions, settling within days — is the classic presentation of a load-related musculotendinous irritation pattern, not an acute structural injury. That's a working hypothesis from a history alone, so here's the honest framing and the plan.

Most likely contributors

  • Shoulder: rotator-cuff / subacromial irritation from pressing with a pronated barbell grip and wide elbow flare, plus overhead work and wide-grip pulldowns.
  • Elbows (both sides): the flexor–pronator mass (medial) and wrist extensor origin (lateral) working overtime — supinated curls, straight-bar work, heavy gripping in rows, RDLs and pull-ups, plus a sudden jump in volume.
  • Systemic factor: going from inconsistent training to a full program concentrates a lot of new load into tendons that adapt more slowly than muscle and nerve. Tendons need 6–12 weeks to remodel; your enthusiasm is faster than your collagen.

Why it settles in a few days (and what that tells us)

Pain that resolves within days of unloading is a strong signal of a reactive, load-driven process rather than a degenerative or structural one — the tendon or musculotendinous junction gets irritated, inflammation and sensitisation follow, then settle when the load drops. That's exactly the population where load management plus progressive loading works, and where rest alone fails: full rest deconditions the tendon and guarantees the pain returns when you reload.

So the plan is not "stop" — it's change the levers (grip, angle, range) and keep loading within a pain envelope.

Pain rules (the ones that matter)

  • ≤3/10 during a set is acceptable if it eases as the set goes on and is gone within 24 h. Use it as a dial, not a light switch.
  • Worse the next morning, or lasting >24 h → cut the load 20–30%, halve the sets, or swap the exercise.
  • Never train through sharp, catching or radiating pain.
  • Tendons respond to slow, heavy-ish, controlled loading: 3 s down, 1 s pause, smooth up. Not bouncing; not endless light reps.
See a clinician in person (not a website) if you get: night pain that wakes you, weakness or a dropping grip, numbness or tingling into the hand, swelling, locking or catching, pain at rest, pain after a single specific incident/trauma, or no improvement after 6–8 weeks of the plan below. As a med student: also get an actual shoulder exam done by someone who does them daily — the tests take three minutes and change the program completely.

The prehab block — 6–8 minutes, 2–3×/week

Do this at the end of Upper A, Upper B and (ideally) once more on a rest day. Tick each movement per session in the boxes below; it saves automatically.

Returning to barbell work

The goal isn't to avoid barbells forever — it's to earn them back with a tolerant tendon.

  1. Weeks 1–3: neutral-grip everything, straps on all pulls, prehab 3×/week, RIR 3 on pressing. Pain-free sets are the only ones that count.
  2. Weeks 4–6: if pain is gone and stays gone, reintroduce one barbell movement at a time — typically the flat press or a Swiss-bar version — at 60% of your previous load. Keep the prehab.
  3. Weeks 7–10: add a second barbell lift (e.g. barbell row before back squat, deadlift with straps before deadlift without). Increase 2.5 kg per session while pain stays 0–2/10 and resolves within 24 h.
  4. Any flare: step back one stage for two weeks. Flares are normal and are information about load, not failures of the plan.

Tendon pain typically takes 6–12 weeks to become reliably quiet. Expect a wobbly line, not a straight one.

If the shoulder is the main problem, add

  • Scapular control first: wall slides, prone Y/T raises, serratus punches — 2 × 12 each, controlled.
  • External rotation strength is the protective variable: cable or band ER at the side, 3 × 15, plus the face pulls already in Upper B.
  • Pressing angle ladder: start with landmine/incline path, progress toward flat, overhead last. Overhead pressing is the final skill you earn back, not the first.
  • Sleeping position matters: sleeping on that shoulder or with the arm overhead keeps it irritated. Roll onto your back or the other side.

If the elbows are the main problem, add

  • Medial (inner) pain: slow eccentric pronation/supination with a light hammer or bottle — 3 × 15, 3 s down.
  • Lateral (outer) pain: slow eccentric wrist extension — 3 × 15, 3 s down (or a FlexBar "Tyler twist" if you have one).
  • Isometrics for relief: 3 × 30–45 s holds at a low-mid intensity; isometric loading has an analgesic effect in tendinopathy and is useful on flare days.
  • Grip hygiene: straps for every pulling set, thicker/neutral handles, avoid death-gripping the bar, take the money out of the flexor mass.
Why this doesn't cost you results: none of these substitutions remove training stimulus. Neutral-grip pressing builds the same chest and triceps; machine or hack squats build the same quads; straps and neutral handles change nothing about the muscle's job. The only thing traded away is a joint-provocation you don't need. What would cost you results is a 6-week layoff from an angry tendon — which is exactly what this plan is designed to prevent.
